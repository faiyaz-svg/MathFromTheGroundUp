# How to study

After this page you know which study habits this book is built around, the evidence for each one, and how to fit them into a week.

Some habits feel productive and do little: rereading a chapter, highlighting it, or doing ten problems of the same kind in a row. Others feel slow or frustrating while you do them and leave you remembering far more a week later. Robert Bjork's name for these is desirable difficulties (Bjork 1994). The first four sections below are about habits of that kind, and the book is set up so that each lesson builds them in.

Two summaries of the research are the main sources here. The IES practice guide *Organizing Instruction and Study to Improve Student Learning* (Pashler et al. 2007) makes seven recommendations and grades the evidence for each one as strong, moderate or low. Dunlosky et al. (2013) reviewed ten common study techniques and rated how useful each one is as high, moderate or low. Full references are in [Sources](#sources) at the bottom of the page.

## Try the problem before you read the solution

When you read a solution first, you are only checking whether each line makes sense. That is much easier than producing the lines yourself, and it gives you a false idea of how well you could do the problem alone. So for every practice problem, write an attempt before you open the solution, even if it is only a first line or a guess at the method.

A failed attempt still helps. Richland, Kornell and Kao (2009) had students answer questions about a passage before they read it. Those students remembered the tested material better than students who were given that time for extra reading instead, even on the questions they had failed to answer at first. The quick check at the top of each lesson works the same way, because you try three problems before you read the lesson. The IES guide recommends these pre-questions (its Recommendation 5a), although it rates the evidence for them as low.

Don't overdo it on brand-new material. If you still have no idea how to start after ten minutes or so, read the solution, close it, and solve the problem again on your own. The section on [worked examples](#worked-examples-for-new-topics) below explains why that is the right move for a new topic.

## Retrieval practice beats rereading

**Retrieval practice** is studying by recalling material from memory: answering a question, writing a definition with the book closed, doing a problem without notes. In plain words, you pull the idea out of your head instead of putting it in again. Dunlosky et al. call it practice testing.

Roediger and Karpicke (2006) had students study short prose passages and then either reread them or take a recall test on them. On a test five minutes later, the rereaders did better. On tests two days and one week later, the students who had taken recall tests remembered more, even though rereading had made students more confident. The IES guide rates the evidence for using quizzes to bring material back (Recommendation 5b) as strong, and Dunlosky et al. rate practice testing as high utility and rereading as low.

In this book, the quick checks, practice problems, diagnostics and Anki decks are all retrieval practice. One more habit costs about two minutes: after a lesson, close it, write the definitions and the steps of the method on a blank page, then compare with the lesson and fix what you missed.

## Space it out

**Spacing** means spreading the study of one topic over several sessions on different days, instead of doing it all in one sitting. Dunlosky et al. call it distributed practice. For the same total time, spaced study is remembered for much longer.

Dunlosky et al. rate spacing as high utility, the only technique besides practice testing to get that rating. The IES guide's first recommendation is to review material after a delay of several weeks to several months after first learning it (moderate evidence). In the first experiment of Rohrer and Taylor (2007), students who spread their practice problems over two sessions did much better on a test a week later than students who did all of them in one session.

The book builds spacing in for you. Anki brings each card back after a growing gap. The challenge tier of every lesson includes a problem from an earlier lesson. The weekly routine below has you redo problems from the week before. When you finish a folder, take its diagnostic again a few weeks later.

## Mix problem types

**Interleaving** means mixing different kinds of problems in one practice session, instead of doing a block of one kind and then a block of the next. A block is easier, because every problem uses the method you just read. Interleaved problems make you decide which method fits, and that decision is the hard part of an exam question or a proof.

Rohrer and Taylor (2007) taught 18 college students to find the volumes of four unfamiliar solids. One group practised in blocks, with four problems on one solid followed by four on the next. The other group did the same problems interleaved. During practice, the block group scored higher, 89% against 60%. On a test one week later, the interleaved group scored 63% and the block group 20%. This was a small study, and Dunlosky et al. rate interleaved practice as moderate utility.

Expect interleaving to feel worse while you do it: you make more mistakes and move more slowly. In the study above, the group that did worse in practice did far better on the test. In this book, each lesson's challenge tier mixes in an earlier lesson, the diagnostics mix a whole folder, and 04-proofs-and-logic ends with a mixed proof practice<!-- link: ../04-proofs-and-logic/07-putting-it-together/02-mixed-proof-practice.md --> lesson.

## Worked examples for new topics

A **worked example** is a problem with its full solution written out step by step. For a topic that is new to you, studying worked examples teaches faster than solving problems from scratch, because searching for a method uses up attention you could spend learning the steps. This is called the worked-example effect.

In one of the first studies of it (Sweller and Cooper 1985), students in grades 8 and 9 learning algebra alternated between studying a worked example and solving a similar problem, four pairs in all. Another group solved all eight problems on their own. The alternating group took less time and did better on the test afterward. The IES guide turns this into its Recommendation 2, to alternate worked examples with problems to solve, and rates the evidence as moderate.

The benefit shrinks as you learn the topic, and it can reverse. Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler and Sweller (2003) called this the **expertise reversal effect**: guidance that helps a beginner can slow down someone who already knows the material, and sometimes leaves them learning less. In plain words, examples you don't need get in the way. That is why every lesson starts with a quick check. If you get all three right, the worked examples would mostly cost you time, so you skip to the practice problems.

The lessons follow the same pattern from example to example. Example 1 is explained at every step, later examples have less commentary, and one leaves its last step for you. The IES guide also recommends this gradual fading from examples to problems as students gain skill.

## Explain each step to yourself

**Self-explanation** means saying to yourself, as you read each line of a solution or proof, why that line follows from the ones before it. Chi et al. (1989) found that the students who learned most from worked physics examples were the ones who explained the steps to themselves as they read. Chi et al. (1994) then asked a group of grade 8 students to explain each line of a text on the human circulatory system to themselves, and they understood it better than a group who read the text twice.

The IES guide's Recommendation 7, to ask and answer deep "why" and "how" questions, is one of only two it rates as having strong evidence. Dunlosky et al. rate self-explanation as moderate utility.

To do it, finish the sentence "This step is allowed because ..." for each line. For example, "divide both sides by $x$" is allowed only when $x \ne 0$, so explaining the step makes you notice the case $x = 0$, which the step leaves out. In a proof, name the definition, hypothesis or earlier result that each line uses, and write it in the margin if that helps.

## Use the diagnostics well

Right after you study something, you are a poor judge of whether you know it. The IES guide (Recommendation 6) says that most learners, without training, overestimate how well they know material they have just studied. It recommends judging what you know after a delay, with the answer hidden, and using quizzes to find what still needs study. It rates the evidence as low, mostly because the studies were done in laboratories rather than classrooms.

The diagnostics are built for this.

1. Take the diagnostic cold, with no notes, and without studying the folder first.
2. Mark each answer right, wrong or guessed. A guess counts as wrong, even when it turns out right.
3. Do the lessons behind the wrong and guessed questions. Each question names its lesson.
4. A few weeks after you finish a folder, take its diagnostic again. Anything you miss the second time goes back on your list.

A low score only tells you which lessons to do, and finding that out is the whole point of the quiz.

## A weekly routine

This routine assumes four or five study sessions a week, each of about 30 to 60 minutes. Change the numbers to fit your term.

### Each session

1. Do your Anki reviews first. They take a few minutes.
2. Take the quick check of the next lesson on your list. If you get all three right, go straight to its practice problems and do the challenge tier. If those go well, tick the lesson and move on.
3. Otherwise, work through the lesson. Build an example and a non-example for each definition, read the worked examples with the solution covered, and attempt each practice problem before you open its solution.
4. Close the lesson, write its definitions and method from memory, and check them against the page.

### Once a week

1. Redo three or four problems from last week's lessons, mixed together, without looking at the lessons first.
2. Redo one problem you got wrong earlier, starting from a blank page.
3. Update the [progress page](../progress.md) and choose next week's lessons.

### When a course needs a topic now

Go to that lesson first. Use its quick check and prerequisite links to find the gaps behind it, and fill those before the lesson itself. The rest of the routine carries on around it.
