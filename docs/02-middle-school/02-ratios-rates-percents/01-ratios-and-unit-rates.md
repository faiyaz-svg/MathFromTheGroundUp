# Ratios and unit rates

After this lesson you can write and simplify a ratio, split an amount in a given ratio, find a unit rate, change the units of a rate by cancelling them, and use unit prices to see which package is the better buy.

Before this: [fractions and equivalent fractions](../../01-elementary/09-fractions-and-equivalent-fractions.md), [multiplying and dividing decimals](../../01-elementary/13-multiplying-and-dividing-decimals.md) and [measurement and unit conversion](../../01-elementary/15-measurement-and-unit-conversion.md).

## Quick check

Try these first. If you get all three right, skip to [Practice](#practice).

1. Write the ratio $18:24$ in simplest form, and write $0.6:1.5$ as a ratio of whole numbers in simplest form.
2. Split \$240 between two people in the ratio $3:5$.
3. A 750 g bag of rice costs \$4.29 and a 2 kg bag costs \$10.49. Which bag has the lower price per 100 g?

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Divide both terms by their GCF, 6, to get $3:4$. For the second ratio, multiply both terms by 10 to get $6:15$, then divide both by 3 to get $2:5$.
2. The ratio has $3 + 5 = 8$ parts, so one part is $240 \div 8 = 30$ dollars. The shares are $3 \times 30 = 90$ and $5 \times 30 = 150$, that is, \$90 and \$150.
3. The 2 kg bag. The small bag holds 7.5 lots of 100 g, so it costs $4.29 \div 7.5 = 0.572$, about \$0.57 per 100 g. The big bag holds 20 lots, so it costs $10.49 \div 20 = 0.5245$, about \$0.52 per 100 g.

</details>

## The idea

A can of frozen juice concentrate says to mix it with 3 cans of water. If you follow the label, the drink tastes the same whether you make one can's worth or a whole jug: 2 cans of concentrate need 6 cans of water, and 5 cans need 15. What stays fixed is the comparison "3 cans of water for every can of concentrate". That comparison is a ratio, written $1:3$.

Scaling a ratio means multiplying. Suppose you add one more can of concentrate and one more can of water to the label's batch, for 2 cans of concentrate and 4 of water. The drink comes out stronger, because concentrate is now a third of the drink instead of a quarter. A ratio compares two amounts by division, so to keep it the same, the same factor has to multiply both amounts.

A ratio of two different kinds of quantity is a rate. A car that goes 450 km on 36 L of gas travels $450 \div 36 = 12.5$ km per litre. Rates are what make comparisons fair when the amounts differ. A grocery shelf label does this for you when it shows the price per 100 g next to the price, so that a 340 g bag and a 925 g bag can be compared directly, as in Example 1.

## Definitions

Let $a$ and $b$ be numbers. The **ratio** of $a$ to $b$, written $a:b$ and read "$a$ to $b$", compares $a$ with $b$ by division. When $b \ne 0$, its value is the quotient $\frac{a}{b}$, so the ratio can also be written as that fraction. The numbers $a$ and $b$ are the **terms** of the ratio.

In plain words, $a:b$ says "for every $a$ of the first thing there are $b$ of the second". The order matters: the juice is $1:3$ concentrate to water, while $3:1$ would be a drink that is three quarters concentrate. A ratio can also have more than two terms, such as a concrete mix of cement, sand and gravel in the ratio $1:2:3$.

A **part-to-part ratio** compares two parts of a whole, and a **part-to-whole ratio** compares one part with the whole. The juice ratio $1:3$ is part-to-part. The drink is $1 + 3 = 4$ cans, so the part-to-whole ratio of concentrate to drink is $1:4$, and concentrate is $\frac{1}{4}$ of the drink.

Multiplying or dividing every term of a ratio by the same nonzero number gives an **equivalent ratio**, one that makes the same comparison. So $1:3$, $2:6$ and $0.5:1.5$ are equivalent. Formally, when $b \ne 0$ and $d \ne 0$, the ratios $a:b$ and $c:d$ are equivalent exactly when $\frac{a}{b} = \frac{c}{d}$, which is the test for [equivalent fractions](../../01-elementary/09-fractions-and-equivalent-fractions.md#definitions).

A ratio is in **simplest form** when its terms are whole numbers with no common factor other than 1. The ratio $2:5$ is in simplest form. The ratios $6:15$ (common factor 3) and $0.4:1$ (a term that isn't whole) are equivalent to it but are not in simplest form.

A **rate** is a ratio of two quantities measured in different units, such as 450 km per 36 L or \$8.99 per 340 g. The word "per" means "for each", and in a unit it becomes a fraction bar: km/L means $\frac{\text{km}}{\text{L}}$. A **unit rate** is a rate for one unit of the second quantity, such as 12.5 km per litre or \$19 per hour.

A **unit price** is the price for a fixed amount of what you buy, such as dollars per kilogram. Grocery shelf labels in Canada often give it per 100 g or per 100 mL, and sometimes per kilogram or per litre. A price per 100 g is strictly a rate for 100 g rather than for one gram, but it compares packages in exactly the same way.

## How to do it

### Simplifying a ratio

1. If the terms measure the same kind of thing, write them in the same unit, then drop the unit. For example, 45 min to 2 h is 45 min to 120 min, which is $45:120$.
2. If a term is a decimal or a fraction, multiply every term by the same number to make them all whole: a power of ten for decimals, a common denominator for fractions.
3. Divide every term by the GCF of the terms, as in [factors, multiples and primes](../../01-elementary/08-factors-multiples-and-primes.md).
4. Check that the new ratio has the same value as the old one, by dividing the first term by the second in each.

The GCF of 45 and 120 is 15, so $45:120$ simplifies to $3:8$. Check: $45 \div 120 = 0.375$ and $3 \div 8 = 0.375$.

### Ratio tables

A ratio table lists equivalent ratios, one per row. Each row comes from another by multiplying or dividing every entry by the same number, so you can build up to the amount you need. Here is the juice from [The idea](#the-idea), with a column for the whole drink:

| Concentrate (cans) | Water (cans) | Drink (cans) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 3 | 4 |
| 2 | 6 | 8 |
| 0.5 | 1.5 | 2 |
| 2.5 | 7.5 | 10 |

How much concentrate makes 10 cans of drink? Halve the first row to get the third (2 cans of drink), then multiply the third row by 5 to get the last. You need 2.5 cans of concentrate and 7.5 cans of water. Adding two rows also works, because it combines two batches of the same mix: the second and third rows add up to the last one.

### Splitting an amount in a given ratio

1. Add the terms of the ratio to get the total number of parts.
2. Divide the amount by the number of parts to get one part.
3. Multiply one part by each term to get each share.
4. Check that the shares add up to the amount and are in the given ratio.

### Finding and comparing unit rates

1. Write each rate as a fraction with its units, with the quantity you want "per" on the bottom. A price of \$8.99 for 340 g is $\frac{8.99\ \text{dollars}}{340\ \text{g}}$.
2. Put the rates you are comparing in the same units. If one label is per 100 g and the other is per kilogram, convert one of them.
3. Divide the numerator by the denominator to get the amount for one unit, or for 100 g if that is the unit you chose.
4. Compare the unit rates. Round once, at the end. For prices, the lower unit price is the better buy.
5. Check with an estimate, or by scaling one package to the size of the other.

### Converting the units of a rate

The units of a rate form a fraction, so they convert with the conversion factors from [measurement and unit conversion](../../01-elementary/15-measurement-and-unit-conversion.md#how-to-do-it): choose each factor so that the unit you don't want cancels. A flyer that advertises chicken at \$2.99 per pound needs one factor, with pounds on top, since 1 lb = 0.45359237 kg:

$$
2.99\ \frac{\text{dollars}}{\cancel{\text{lb}}} \times \frac{1\ \cancel{\text{lb}}}{0.45359237\ \text{kg}} = 6.5918\ldots\ \frac{\text{dollars}}{\text{kg}}.
$$

So \$2.99/lb is about \$6.59/kg. Check with the rough value: a kilogram is about 2.2 pounds, and $2.2 \times 2.99 = 6.578$, close to 6.59. The price per kilogram is the bigger number because a kilogram is more chicken than a pound.

## Worked examples

<div class="example" markdown>

### Example 1: two bags of coffee

A 340 g bag of coffee costs \$8.99, and a 925 g bag of the same coffee costs \$21.49. Which is the better buy, and by how much per 100 g?

Step 1: write each price as a rate with its units:

$$
\frac{8.99\ \text{dollars}}{340\ \text{g}} \qquad \text{and} \qquad \frac{21.49\ \text{dollars}}{925\ \text{g}}.
$$

Step 2: both masses are already in grams. Shelf labels use 100 g, so count each bag in lots of 100 g. Dividing by 100 moves the decimal point two places left, so 340 g is 3.4 lots and 925 g is 9.25 lots.

Step 3: divide each price by its number of lots:

$$
8.99 \div 3.4 = 2.6441\ldots \qquad \text{and} \qquad 21.49 \div 9.25 = 2.3232\ldots
$$

Step 4: rounded to the cent, the small bag costs \$2.64 per 100 g and the big bag costs \$2.32 per 100 g. The big bag is the better buy, by about 32 cents per 100 g.

<div class="warning" markdown>

Watch out at Step 3: dividing the other way, $340 \div 8.99 = 37.8$, gives grams per dollar. That is a correct rate too, but with it the bigger number is the better buy, so the comparison flips. Keep the price on top, as in Step 1, and the lower unit price wins.

</div>

Step 5: check by scaling. The big bag holds $925 \div 340 \approx 2.72$ times as much coffee as the small one. At the small bag's price, that much coffee would cost about $2.72 \times 8.99 \approx 24.5$ dollars, and the big bag costs only \$21.49, so it is the better buy, as Step 4 found.

</div>

<div class="example" markdown>

### Example 2: splitting the rent

Three roommates share an apartment with a rent of \$2,850 a month. They split it in the ratio of their bedroom areas, which are $12\ \text{m}^2$, $10\ \text{m}^2$ and $8\ \text{m}^2$. What does each one pay?

The GCF of 12, 10 and 8 is 2, so the ratio $12:10:8$ simplifies to $6:5:4$. Simplifying first is optional, but it keeps the numbers small.

Steps 1 to 3: there are $6 + 5 + 4 = 15$ parts, so one part is $2850 \div 15 = 190$ dollars. The shares are $6 \times 190 = 1140$, $5 \times 190 = 950$ and $4 \times 190 = 760$, that is, \$1,140, \$950 and \$760.

Step 4: $1140 + 950 + 760 = 2850$, and dividing each share by 190 gives back $6:5:4$.

</div>

<div class="example" markdown>

### Example 3: a split that doesn't come out in whole cents

Three friends split a \$100 grocery bill in the ratio $2:3:4$, by the number of meals each one will eat. What does each pay?

Steps 1 and 2: there are $2 + 3 + 4 = 9$ parts, so one part is $100 \div 9 = 11.111\ldots$ dollars, a decimal that never ends.

Step 3: the exact shares are $22.222\ldots$, $33.333\ldots$ and $44.444\ldots$ dollars. Rounded to the cent, they are \$22.22, \$33.33 and \$44.44.

Step 4 finds a problem: $22.22 + 33.33 + 44.44 = 99.99$. Each share was rounded down by a fraction of a cent, and together they lost a whole cent, which somebody has to pay. A fair rule gives the extra cent to the share that lost the most in rounding. The third share dropped $0.444\ldots$ of a cent, more than the others ($0.222\ldots$ and $0.333\ldots$), so it becomes \$44.45. The shares \$22.22, \$33.33 and \$44.45 add up to \$100.00.

This untidy answer is not a mistake. Whenever the number of parts doesn't divide the amount evenly in cents, the rounded shares can miss the total by a cent or two. [In code](#in-code) splits money the same way a program would.

</div>

<div class="example" markdown>

### Example 4: gas for a road trip (finish it yourself)

Your car uses 7.2 L of gas per 100 km, and gas costs \$1.52 per litre. What does the gas cost for the 450 km drive from Toronto to Ottawa?

This is a chain of two rates, so set it up with conversion factors, as in [Converting the units of a rate](#converting-the-units-of-a-rate). Start with the distance, and choose each factor so that the unit before it cancels. The fuel rate has kilometres on the bottom, to cancel the kilometres in 450 km, and the price has litres on the bottom, to cancel the litres in the fuel rate:

$$
450\ \cancel{\text{km}} \times \frac{7.2\ \cancel{\text{L}}}{100\ \cancel{\text{km}}} \times \frac{1.52\ \text{dollars}}{1\ \cancel{\text{L}}}.
$$

Every unit except dollars cancels, so the answer is in dollars. Now work out the litres used and then the cost, and check with an estimate.

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Finish</summary>

The litres used are $450 \times 7.2 \div 100 = 32.4$ L, and the cost is $32.4 \times 1.52 = 49.248$, about \$49.25.

Check: 450 km is 4.5 lots of 100 km at about 7 L each, which is about 31.5 L. At about \$1.50 a litre, 32 L costs about \$48, close to \$49.25.

</details>

## Edge cases and common mistakes

<div class="warning" markdown>

Watch out for the order of the terms. A recipe with flour and sugar in the ratio $3:1$ has three times as much flour as sugar, and $1:3$ would be a very different cake. Say the ratio in words first, with the quantities named ("3 cups of flour to 1 cup of sugar"), then write the numbers in that order.

</div>

<div class="warning" markdown>

Watch out for part-to-part and part-to-whole. A lecture has 3 economics students for every 5 CS students. The economics students are not $\frac{3}{5}$ of the lecture: out of every $3 + 5 = 8$ students, 3 study economics, so they are $\frac{3}{8}$ of it, which is 37.5%. The fraction $\frac{3}{5}$ compares the economics students with the CS students, not with the whole lecture.

</div>

<div class="warning" markdown>

Watch out: adding the same number to every term does not give an equivalent ratio. Going from $1:3$ to $2:4$ adds 1 to each term, and the drink gets stronger, since $\frac{1}{3} \ne \frac{2}{4}$. To double the recipe, multiply both terms by 2, which gives $2:6$.

</div>

<div class="warning" markdown>

Watch out for mixed units. The ratio of 45 minutes to 2 hours is not $45:2$. In one unit it is $45:120$, which simplifies to $3:8$. Check the units of shelf labels the same way before you compare them: \$1.29/100 g and \$12.20/kg are in different units, and the second one is \$1.22/100 g (Practice problem 5).

</div>

Zero needs a little care. A ratio such as $0:5$ is fine: there is none of the first thing. A ratio $5:0$ can be written down, but it has no value as a fraction, since $\frac{5}{0}$ is undefined, and a rate "per 0 units" means nothing.

Decimals and fractions inside a ratio are fine as well, since $1.5:2$ makes the same comparison as $3:4$. Simplest form asks for whole numbers only so that everyone writes the same ratio the same way.

## Tricks and shortcuts

For a price per 100 g, divide the price by the mass with its decimal point moved two places left. A 650 g tub of yogurt at \$3.99 costs $3.99 \div 6.5 \approx 0.61$, about \$0.61 per 100 g. For the price per kilogram, move the point three places instead: $3.99 \div 0.65 \approx 6.14$ dollars per kg.

To compare two packages without working out either unit price, scale one package to the size of the other, as in the check for Example 1. Take a 500 g box of cereal at \$3.49 and a 1.2 kg box at \$7.99. The big box holds 2.4 small boxes' worth, and $2.4 \times 3.49 = 8.376$, so 1.2 kg at the small box's price would cost about \$8.38. The big box is cheaper.

From a part-to-part ratio $a:b$, the two parts are $\frac{a}{a+b}$ and $\frac{b}{a+b}$ of the whole. For $3:5$, they are $\frac{3}{8}$ and $\frac{5}{8}$.

A rate can be flipped. The rates 12.5 km per litre and 0.08 L per kilometre state the same fact, since $1 \div 12.5 = 0.08$. Canadian fuel ratings use litres per 100 km, which for this car is $100 \div 12.5 = 8$ L/100 km. With kilometres per litre a higher number is better, and with litres per 100 km a lower number is better.

The units tell you whether to multiply or divide. A speed times a time is a distance, because the hours cancel:

$$
80\ \frac{\text{km}}{\cancel{\text{h}}} \times 3.5\ \cancel{\text{h}} = 280\ \text{km}.
$$

## In code

A money split has to add up exactly, as Example 3 showed. Programs that handle money usually count in whole cents, round every share down, and then hand out the leftover cents one at a time to the shares that lost the most. This is called the largest remainder method.

<details class="code" markdown>
<summary>In code</summary>

```python
def split(total_cents, ratio):
    parts = sum(ratio)
    shares = [total_cents * r // parts for r in ratio]   # round each share down
    leftover = total_cents - sum(shares)                 # cents not handed out yet
    lost = [total_cents * r % parts for r in ratio]      # what each share lost
    order = sorted(range(len(ratio)), key=lambda i: lost[i], reverse=True)
    for i in order[:leftover]:
        shares[i] += 1
    return shares

print(split(10000, [2, 3, 4]))    # $100 in the ratio 2:3:4: [2222, 3333, 4445]
print(split(285000, [6, 5, 4]))   # the rent from Example 2: [114000, 95000, 76000]
print(split(10000, [1, 1, 1]))    # $100 three ways: [3334, 3333, 3333]
```

The operator `//` divides and rounds down, and `%` gives the remainder, as in [long division](../../01-elementary/07-long-division.md). The exact share is `total_cents * r / parts` cents, so the remainder `lost[i]` measures how much share `i` lost by rounding down. In the last line all three shares lose the same amount, and the extra cent goes to the first one.

</details>

## FAQ

### Is a ratio the same as a fraction?

Almost. Every ratio $a:b$ with $b \ne 0$ has the value $\frac{a}{b}$, and equivalent ratios have equal values, so for comparing ratios you can treat them as fractions. But the value of a part-to-part ratio is not a fraction of the whole: in a $3:5$ ratio, the first part is $\frac{3}{8}$ of the whole, not $\frac{3}{5}$. A ratio can also have three or more terms, such as $6:5:4$, and no single fraction can hold that.

### What is the difference between a ratio and a rate?

A rate is a ratio whose two quantities have different units, so the units stay in the answer, as in 12.5 km/L. When both quantities have the same unit, the units cancel and the ratio is a plain number: 45 min to 120 min is $3:8$, or 0.375, with no unit. Some books call both of them ratios. Economics also says "rate" for many percents, such as the unemployment rate and the inflation rate.

### Is the bigger package always cheaper per gram?

Usually, but not always. A sale or a multi-buy offer can make the small package cheaper per gram, which is why the unit price on the label is worth reading. And a lower unit price only saves money if you use what you buy: a big bag of coffee that goes stale before you finish it can cost more per cup than a small one.

## Practice

### Warm-up

1\. Write each ratio in simplest form: (a) the width to the height of a screen that is 1920 by 1080 pixels, (b) $2.5:4$, (c) 45 minutes to 2 hours, (d) $\frac{3}{4}:\frac{2}{3}$.

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Solution</summary>

- (a) The GCF of 1920 and 1080 is 120, so $1920:1080 = 16:9$, the usual widescreen shape.
- (b) Multiply by 10 to clear the decimal: $25:40$. The GCF is 5, so the answer is $5:8$.
- (c) Write both in minutes: $45:120$. The GCF is 15, so the answer is $3:8$.
- (d) Multiply both terms by 12, a common denominator: $\frac{3}{4} \times 12 = 9$ and $\frac{2}{3} \times 12 = 8$, so the answer is $9:8$.

Check by dividing: $1920 \div 1080 = 1.777\ldots = 16 \div 9$, $2.5 \div 4 = 0.625 = 5 \div 8$, $45 \div 120 = 0.375 = 3 \div 8$, and $\frac{3}{4} \div \frac{2}{3} = \frac{9}{8}$.

</details>

2\. A jar holds red and blue marbles in the ratio $4:7$. (a) What fraction of the marbles are red? (b) The jar holds 66 marbles. How many of each colour are there? (c) Write the ratio of blue marbles to all the marbles.

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Solution</summary>

(a) Out of every $4 + 7 = 11$ marbles, 4 are red, so $\frac{4}{11}$ of the marbles are red.

(b) One part is $66 \div 11 = 6$ marbles. There are $4 \times 6 = 24$ red marbles and $7 \times 6 = 42$ blue ones.

(c) Blue to all is a part-to-whole ratio, $7:11$.

Check: $24 + 42 = 66$, and $24:42$ divided by 6 is $4:7$.

</details>

3\. Find each unit rate: (a) \$342 for 18 hours of work, in dollars per hour, (b) 273 km in 3.5 hours, in km/h, (c) 34.2 L of gas for 450 km, in litres per 100 km.

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Solution</summary>

- (a) $342 \div 18 = 19$, so \$19.00 per hour.
- (b) $273 \div 3.5 = 78$, so 78 km/h.
- (c) The distance is 4.5 lots of 100 km, so the rate is $34.2 \div 4.5 = 7.6$ L/100 km.

Check by multiplying back: $19 \times 18 = 342$, $78 \times 3.5 = 273$ and $7.6 \times 4.5 = 34.2$.

</details>

### Standard

4\. (a) Split \$1,500 in the ratio $2:3:7$. (b) A different amount is split in the ratio $2:3:7$, and the largest share is \$560. Find the other two shares and the total.

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Solution</summary>

(a) There are $2 + 3 + 7 = 12$ parts, and one part is $1500 \div 12 = 125$ dollars. The shares are $2 \times 125 = 250$, $3 \times 125 = 375$ and $7 \times 125 = 875$ dollars.

(b) Work backwards from the share you know. The largest share is 7 parts, so one part is $560 \div 7 = 80$ dollars. The other shares are $2 \times 80 = 160$ and $3 \times 80 = 240$ dollars, and the total is $12 \times 80 = 960$ dollars.

Check: $250 + 375 + 875 = 1500$, and $160 + 240 + 560 = 960$.

</details>

5\. Two stores sell the same cheddar. The shelf label at store A says \$1.29/100 g. Store B sells a 450 g block for \$5.49, and its label says \$12.20/kg. (a) Which store is cheaper? (b) How much would you save on 450 g by buying at the cheaper store?

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Solution</summary>

(a) Put both prices in the same unit. A kilogram is 1000 g, which is 10 lots of 100 g, so \$12.20/kg is $12.20 \div 10 = 1.22$ dollars per 100 g. Store B is cheaper, by 7 cents per 100 g.

(b) At store A, 450 g costs $1.29 \times 4.5 = 5.805$, about \$5.81. Store B charges \$5.49, so you save $5.81 - 5.49 = 0.32$ dollars, about 32 cents.

Check: store B's label is right, since $5.49 \div 0.45 = 12.20$. And 7 cents on each of 4.5 lots of 100 g is $0.07 \times 4.5 = 0.315$ dollars, about 32 cents.

</details>

6\. One job pays \$23.50 an hour for 37.5 hours a week. Another pays a salary of \$46,000 a year for the same hours. Assuming 52 paid weeks a year, which job pays more per hour, and by how much a year?

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Solution</summary>

Convert the salary to dollars per hour, choosing each factor so that years and weeks cancel:

$$
\frac{46000\ \text{dollars}}{1\ \cancel{\text{year}}} \times \frac{1\ \cancel{\text{year}}}{52\ \cancel{\text{weeks}}} \times \frac{1\ \cancel{\text{week}}}{37.5\ \text{h}} = 23.589\ldots\ \frac{\text{dollars}}{\text{h}}.
$$

So the salary is about \$23.59 an hour, which is more than \$23.50. Over a year, the hourly job pays $23.50 \times 37.5 \times 52 = 45{,}825$ dollars, so the salary pays $46{,}000 - 45{,}825 = 175$ dollars more, about 9 cents an hour.

Check: a year of work is $37.5 \times 52 = 1950$ hours, and $175 \div 1950 \approx 0.09$, which matches the 9 cents.

</details>

### Challenge

7\. Juice concentrate is mixed with water in the ratio $1:4$. You have made 2 L of drink, and it tastes weak. How much concentrate should you add to change the mix to $1:3$, without adding any water?

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Solution</summary>

The 2 L of drink is $1 + 4 = 5$ parts, so one part is 0.4 L. The drink holds 0.4 L of concentrate and $4 \times 0.4 = 1.6$ L of water.

The water stays at 1.6 L. In a $1:3$ mix the concentrate is a third of the water, so it must be $1.6 \div 3 = 0.5333\ldots$ L. You have 0.4 L, so add

$$
0.5333\ldots - 0.4 = 0.1333\ldots\ \text{L},
$$

which is $133\frac{1}{3}$ mL, about 133 mL. The answer doesn't come out even, and that is fine; a kitchen measure gets close enough.

Check with exact fractions: the new mix has $\frac{8}{15}$ L of concentrate and $\frac{24}{15}$ L of water, and $8:24 = 1:3$.

</details>

8\. (Reviews [multiplying and dividing fractions](../../01-elementary/11-multiplying-and-dividing-fractions.md).) A recipe for 6 people uses $\frac{3}{4}$ cup of sugar and $2\frac{1}{4}$ cups of flour. (a) What is the ratio of sugar to flour, in simplest form? (b) How much flour do you need to make the recipe for 10 people?

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Solution</summary>

(a) Write the mixed number as a fraction, $2\frac{1}{4} = \frac{9}{4}$. The ratio is $\frac{3}{4}:\frac{9}{4}$. Multiply both terms by 4 to get $3:9$, and divide both by 3 to get $1:3$.

(b) Going from 6 people to 10 multiplies every amount by $\frac{10}{6} = \frac{5}{3}$. The flour becomes

$$
\frac{9}{4} \times \frac{5}{3} = \frac{45}{12} = \frac{15}{4} = 3\tfrac{3}{4}\ \text{cups}.
$$

Check: the sugar becomes $\frac{3}{4} \times \frac{5}{3} = \frac{5}{4}$ cups, and $\frac{5}{4}:\frac{15}{4} = 5:15 = 1:3$, the same ratio as before. By unit rate instead, the flour per person is $\frac{9}{4} \div 6 = \frac{3}{8}$ cup, and $10 \times \frac{3}{8} = \frac{15}{4}$ cups.

</details>
