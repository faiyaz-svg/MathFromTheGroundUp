# Properties of operations

After this lesson you can name the commutative, associative, identity, zero and distributive properties, use them to turn a calculation into one you can do in your head, and give the property behind each step.

Before this: [addition and subtraction](02-addition-and-subtraction.md) and [multiplication and times tables](03-multiplication-and-times-tables.md).

## Quick check

Try these first. If you get all three right, skip to [Practice](#practice).

1. Work out $4 \times 19 \times 25$ in your head, and name the properties that let you do it the easy way.
2. Work out $6 \times 99$ with the distributive property.
3. Is $(32 \div 8) \div 2$ equal to $32 \div (8 \div 2)$? What does the answer tell you about division?

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. 1,900. Swap the 19 and the 25 (commutative property) and regroup (associative property) so that $4 \times 25 = 100$ is done first: $(4 \times 25) \times 19 = 100 \times 19 = 1{,}900$.
2. 594. Write 99 as $100 - 1$ and distribute: $6 \times 99 = 6 \times 100 - 6 \times 1 = 600 - 6 = 594$.
3. No. The left side is $4 \div 2 = 2$ and the right side is $32 \div 4 = 8$. Division is not associative: moving the brackets can change the answer.

</details>

## The idea

Say you are adding up three prices in your head: \$7, \$8 and \$3. You could go from left to right ($7 + 8 = 15$, then $15 + 3 = 18$). It is quicker to see that 7 and 3 make 10, add those first, and then add the 8. The total is \$18 either way.

You have rearranged sums and products like this for years, and the properties in this lesson are the rules that make it safe. They need names because some rearrangements are not safe. Taking 3 away from 10 leaves 7, but taking 10 away from 3 leaves $-7$, so swapping the two numbers in a subtraction changes the answer.

A picture shows why the safe rules hold. Here are 15 dots in 3 rows of 5. Turn the page on its side and the same dots make 5 rows of 3:

$$
\begin{array}{ccc}
\begin{array}{ccccc}
\bullet & \bullet & \bullet & \bullet & \bullet \\
\bullet & \bullet & \bullet & \bullet & \bullet \\
\bullet & \bullet & \bullet & \bullet & \bullet
\end{array}
& \qquad &
\begin{array}{ccc}
\bullet & \bullet & \bullet \\
\bullet & \bullet & \bullet \\
\bullet & \bullet & \bullet \\
\bullet & \bullet & \bullet \\
\bullet & \bullet & \bullet
\end{array} \\[1ex]
3 \text{ rows of } 5 & & 5 \text{ rows of } 3
\end{array}
$$

No dot was added or removed, so $3 \times 5 = 5 \times 3$. The same turn works for every rectangle of dots, which is why the times table is symmetric and you only had to learn about half of it.

Cutting a rectangle in two explains the most useful rule of all. Split 3 rows of 7 into a block 5 wide and a block 2 wide:

$$
\begin{array}{ccc}
\begin{array}{ccccc}
\bullet & \bullet & \bullet & \bullet & \bullet \\
\bullet & \bullet & \bullet & \bullet & \bullet \\
\bullet & \bullet & \bullet & \bullet & \bullet
\end{array}
& \quad &
\begin{array}{cc}
\bullet & \bullet \\
\bullet & \bullet \\
\bullet & \bullet
\end{array} \\[1ex]
3 \times 5 = 15 & & 3 \times 2 = 6
\end{array}
$$

The two blocks hold all 21 dots, so $3 \times 7 = 3 \times 5 + 3 \times 2 = 15 + 6$. Multiplying a sum gives the same result as multiplying each part and adding. That rule is how you rebuild a times-table fact you have forgotten, and how $7 \times 98$ becomes $700 - 14$ in your head.

## Definitions

In this section $a$, $b$ and $c$ stand for numbers. Every property here holds for all whole numbers, and it keeps holding for the fractions, decimals and negative numbers of later lessons. The usual order applies: work inside brackets first, then multiply, then add and subtract, so $a \times b + a \times c$ means $(a \times b) + (a \times c)$. The full order of operations is in [exponents and order of operations](../02-middle-school/01-integers-and-rational-numbers/04-exponents-and-order-of-operations.md).

### Commutative properties

The **commutative property of addition** says that $a + b = b + a$ for all numbers $a$ and $b$. The **commutative property of multiplication** says that $a \times b = b \times a$ for all numbers $a$ and $b$.

In plain words, you can swap the two numbers in a sum or in a product: $8 + 5 = 5 + 8$, and $3 \times 5 = 5 \times 3$, as the turned rectangle showed.

### Associative properties

The **associative property of addition** says that $(a + b) + c = a + (b + c)$ for all numbers $a$, $b$ and $c$. The **associative property of multiplication** says that $(a \times b) \times c = a \times (b \times c)$ for all numbers $a$, $b$ and $c$.

In plain words, when the same operation appears twice in a row, you can do either one first. Brackets mean "do this first", and here moving them doesn't matter: $(7 + 8) + 3 = 15 + 3$ and $7 + (8 + 3) = 7 + 11$, and both are 18. For multiplication, picture a case of juice boxes with 4 packs, each holding 2 rows of 3. Counting pack by pack gives $4 \times (2 \times 3) = 4 \times 6$, and counting all the rows in the case gives $(4 \times 2) \times 3 = 8 \times 3$. Both are 24.

Since the grouping doesn't matter, a sum such as $7 + 8 + 3$ or a product such as $4 \times 2 \times 3$ needs no brackets. Used together, the commutative and associative properties let you add a list of numbers, or multiply a list of numbers, in whatever order and grouping is easiest. With two different operations, though, moving the brackets does change the answer: $(2 + 3) \times 4 = 20$, but $2 + (3 \times 4) = 14$.

### Identity properties

The **identity property of addition** says that $a + 0 = a$ for every number $a$. The **identity property of multiplication** says that $a \times 1 = a$ for every number $a$. The number 0 is called the identity for addition, and 1 is the identity for multiplication.

In plain words, adding 0 changes nothing, and neither does multiplying by 1: $46 + 0 = 46$, and $46 \times 1 = 46$ because one group of 46 is 46. With the commutative properties, $0 + a = a$ and $1 \times a = a$ as well.

### Zero property

The **zero property of multiplication** says that $a \times 0 = 0$ for every number $a$.

In plain words, 46 groups of nothing is nothing, and so is no groups of 46. Compare it with the identity property: adding 0 leaves a number alone, but multiplying by 0 wipes it out.

### Distributive property

The **distributive property** says that $a \times (b + c) = a \times b + a \times c$ for all numbers $a$, $b$ and $c$. Its full name is the distributive property of multiplication over addition, and it holds over subtraction too: $a \times (b - c) = a \times b - a \times c$.

In plain words, to multiply a sum, multiply each part and add the results, as the split rectangle in [The idea](#the-idea) did with $3 \times (5 + 2)$. The multiplier is handed out, or distributed, to every term inside the brackets. Read from right to left, the same equation pulls out a factor that two products share: $9 \times 47 + 9 \times 53 = 9 \times (47 + 53)$.

### What fails for subtraction and division

A property stated "for all numbers" is false if it fails for even one choice of numbers. A single case that makes a general statement false is a **counterexample**. Each entry in this table is a counterexample:

| Property | Subtraction | Division |
|---|---|---|
| Commutative | $10 - 3 = 7$, but $3 - 10 = -7$ | $12 \div 4 = 3$, but $4 \div 12 = \frac{1}{3}$ |
| Associative | $(10 - 5) - 2 = 3$, but $10 - (5 - 2) = 7$ | $(24 \div 4) \div 2 = 3$, but $24 \div (4 \div 2) = 12$ |
| Identity on both sides | $5 - 0 = 5$, but $0 - 5 = -5$ | $5 \div 1 = 5$, but $1 \div 5 = \frac{1}{5}$ |

So subtraction and division have none of these properties. Order and grouping both matter, and 0 and 1 act as identities only on the right.

Multiplication does distribute over subtraction, as stated above. Division distributes only when a sum is being divided: $(12 + 18) \div 6 = 12 \div 6 + 18 \div 6 = 2 + 3 = 5$. Dividing by a sum doesn't split, since $12 \div (2 + 4) = 2$ while $12 \div 2 + 12 \div 4 = 9$.

Examples can sink a "for all" statement, but they can't prove one. The statement $a + a = a \times a$ holds for $a = 0$ and for $a = 2$, but $3 + 3 = 6$ and $3 \times 3 = 9$, so it is false. A statement about all numbers needs a proof, or an axiom that grants it, and the next section shows what such a proof looks like.

## How to do it

### Rearranging a calculation

These steps turn a long calculation into a short one.

1. Look at the numbers before you start. Find pairs that make round numbers: sums such as $7 + 3$ or $36 + 64$, and products such as $2 \times 5 = 10$, $4 \times 25 = 100$ and $8 \times 125 = 1{,}000$.
2. If the calculation has only additions, or only multiplications, reorder and regroup to bring those pairs together (commutative and associative properties).
3. If a factor is close to a round number, write it as a sum or a difference, such as $98 = 100 - 2$, and distribute (distributive property).
4. If the calculation has a subtraction or a division, don't swap or regroup across it. Keep each minus sign attached to the number after it.
5. Write each step on its own line, with the property that justifies it.
6. Check: work it out another way, or estimate.

### Naming the property behind each step

Step 5 is the habit that pays off later. In a proof, each line must follow from earlier lines by a rule you can name: a definition, an axiom or a result already proved. A chain of equalities with a reason beside each link is the simplest kind of proof, and the properties in this lesson are the first reasons you get to use.

Here is a short proof of that kind. The distributive property, as stated, has the multiplier on the left, as in $a \times (b + c)$. The area model in the next lesson also needs it with the multiplier on the right.

*Theorem.* For all numbers $a$, $b$ and $c$,

$$
(a + b) \times c = a \times c + b \times c.
$$

We know the distributive property and the commutative property of multiplication. We want to multiply out a sum whose multiplier is on the right. The plan is to move $c$ to the left, distribute, and then move $c$ back to the right in each product.

*Proof.*

$$
\begin{aligned}
(a + b) \times c &= c \times (a + b) && \text{commutative property of multiplication} \\
&= c \times a + c \times b && \text{distributive property} \\
&= a \times c + b \times c && \text{commutative property of multiplication, twice}
\end{aligned}
$$

Look back: the second line uses the distributive property with $c$ in the place of $a$, and $a$ and $b$ in the places of $b$ and $c$. That is allowed, because the property holds for all numbers, whatever letters they are called. No step used a particular value, so the result holds for every $a$, $b$ and $c$, and from now on either version counts as the distributive property.

<div class="note" markdown>

These properties are where algebra and proofs start. A university course on the real numbers takes the commutative, associative, identity and distributive properties as axioms, statements accepted without proof, and then proves everything else from them, including facts that seem too basic to need a proof. The zero property is one of those facts: it can be proved from the others, and Practice problem 5 does it. The full list of axioms is in [real numbers, axioms and set notation](../03-high-school/01-algebra-foundations/01-real-numbers-axioms-and-set-notation.md).

</div>

## Worked examples

<div class="example" markdown>

### Example 1: multiplying 25, 37 and 4 in your head

Work out $25 \times 37 \times 4$.

Step 1: look at the numbers. The pair 25 and 4 makes 100, but 37 sits between them.

Step 2: a product with no brackets is worked from left to right, so the calculation as written is $(25 \times 37) \times 4$. To bring 25 and 4 together, move the brackets, swap 37 and 4, and move the brackets back. Step 5 says to name each move:

$$
\begin{aligned}
(25 \times 37) \times 4 &= 25 \times (37 \times 4) && \text{associative property} \\
&= 25 \times (4 \times 37) && \text{commutative property} \\
&= (25 \times 4) \times 37 && \text{associative property} \\
&= 100 \times 37 && \text{since } 25 \times 4 = 100 \\
&= 3{,}700
\end{aligned}
$$

In your head this is one thought: 25 times 4 is 100, and 100 times 37 is 3,700. The written version is slower, but it shows that the shortcut is legal, one property per line.

Step 6: check by going left to right. First $25 \times 37 = 925$ (that's $25 \times 36 + 25 = 900 + 25$), and then $925 \times 4 = 3{,}700$. The answer is the same, but the arithmetic is much harder.

</div>

<div class="example" markdown>

### Example 2: seven times 98

Work out $7 \times 98$. Step 3 applies: 98 is close to 100, so write $98 = 100 - 2$ and distribute over the subtraction.

$$
\begin{aligned}
7 \times 98 &= 7 \times (100 - 2) && \text{since } 98 = 100 - 2 \\
&= 7 \times 100 - 7 \times 2 && \text{distributive property} \\
&= 700 - 14 \\
&= 686
\end{aligned}
$$

As a picture, this is 7 rows of 100 dots with the last 2 dots taken off every row: 700 dots, minus 7 rows of 2.

Check with a different split: $7 \times 98 = 7 \times (90 + 8) = 630 + 56 = 686$. The answer is also a little under $7 \times 100 = 700$, as it should be.

</div>

<div class="example" markdown>

### Example 3: a subtraction that can't be regrouped

Work out $100 - 30 - 20$. Left to right, the answer is $70 - 20 = 50$.

Step 4 says not to regroup across a subtraction, and this is why. Grouping the last two numbers first gives $100 - (30 - 20) = 100 - 10 = 90$, which is wrong. The question takes away 30 and then 20 more, but $100 - (30 - 20)$ takes away only 10 in total.

Two moves are safe. You can collect everything that is taken away, since taking away 30 and then 20 is taking away 50: $100 - 30 - 20 = 100 - (30 + 20) = 50$. And you can reorder, as long as each minus sign stays attached to the number after it. For example, $100 - 30 + 50 = 100 + 50 - 30 = 120$, because the 30 is still taken away and the 50 is still added. Check: left to right, $100 - 30 + 50 = 70 + 50 = 120$.

Algebra makes this precise by treating $-30$ as a number you add: [integer arithmetic and sign rules](../02-middle-school/01-integers-and-rational-numbers/02-integer-arithmetic-and-sign-rules.md).

</div>

## Edge cases and common mistakes

<div class="warning" markdown>

Watch out: the multiplier must reach every term inside the brackets. Writing $3 \times (4 + 5) = 3 \times 4 + 5 = 17$ multiplies only the 4. The right expansion is $3 \times 4 + 3 \times 5 = 12 + 15 = 27$, and the check $3 \times 9 = 27$ agrees. In algebra, where $2x$ means $2 \times x$, the same slip turns $2 \times (x + 3)$ into $2x + 3$ instead of $2x + 6$.

</div>

<div class="warning" markdown>

Watch out: dividing by a sum doesn't split. The division $12 \div (2 + 4)$ is $12 \div 6 = 2$, not $12 \div 2 + 12 \div 4 = 9$. Dividing a sum does split: $(12 + 18) \div 6 = 2 + 3 = 5$. The same mistake comes back with fractions, where $\frac{1}{2 + 4}$ is $\frac{1}{6}$, not $\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{4}$.

</div>

<div class="warning" markdown>

Watch out: 0 behaves differently in addition and in multiplication. Adding 0 changes nothing ($7 + 0 = 7$), but multiplying by 0 gives 0 ($7 \times 0 = 0$, not 7). Division by 0 is a third case: $0 \div 7 = 0$, but $7 \div 0$ has no value at all, for the reason in [division facts and remainders](06-division-facts-and-remainders.md#edge-cases-and-common-mistakes).

</div>

<div class="warning" markdown>

Watch out: in column subtraction, "take the smaller digit from the bigger one" is the commutative mistake in disguise. In $52 - 38$ the ones column needs $2 - 8$. Swapping it to $8 - 2 = 6$ gives 26, but regrouping gives the right answer, 14 ([addition and subtraction](02-addition-and-subtraction.md)). Check: $38 + 14 = 52$, while $38 + 26 = 64$.

</div>

## Tricks and shortcuts

The two names are easy to mix up. To commute is to travel, and in the commutative property the numbers travel past each other and swap places. In the associative property the numbers stay in order and only the brackets move, which changes the numbers that are grouped, or associated, together.

Some products are worth knowing because they make round numbers: $2 \times 5 = 10$, $4 \times 25 = 100$, $2 \times 50 = 100$ and $8 \times 125 = 1{,}000$. Step 1 looks for them.

If one factor is even, you can halve it and double the other: $16 \times 25 = 8 \times 50 = 4 \times 100 = 400$. This is the associative property at work, since $16 \times 25 = (8 \times 2) \times 25 = 8 \times (2 \times 25) = 8 \times 50$. Multiplying by 5 is a special case: $48 \times 5 = 24 \times 10 = 240$.

The distributive property rebuilds a forgotten times-table fact from a neighbour: $9 \times 7 = 10 \times 7 - 1 \times 7 = 63$, and $12 \times 8 = 10 \times 8 + 2 \times 8 = 96$.

### Warm-up

1\. Name the property that makes each equation true.

- (a) $6 \times 9 = 9 \times 6$
- (b) $(3 + 8) + 2 = 3 + (8 + 2)$
- (c) $5 \times (4 + 6) = 5 \times 4 + 5 \times 6$
- (d) $17 \times 1 = 17$
- (e) $0 \times 482 = 0$
- (f) $(2 \times 7) \times 5 = (7 \times 2) \times 5$

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Solution</summary>

- (a) The commutative property of multiplication.
- (b) The associative property of addition.
- (c) The distributive property.
- (d) The identity property of multiplication.
- (e) The zero property of multiplication, together with the commutative property, since the zero property as stated has the 0 on the right.
- (f) The commutative property of multiplication. The 2 and the 7 swap places inside the brackets, and the brackets don't move, so this is not the associative property.

Check by working out both sides: (a) 54, (b) 13, (c) 50, (d) 17, (e) 0 and (f) 70.

</details>

2\. Work out each of these in your head, then write the steps with a property for each one: (a) $4 \times 13 \times 25$, (b) $36 + 57 + 64$, (c) $6 \times 102$.

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Solution</summary>

(a) Bring the 4 and the 25 together:

$$
\begin{aligned}
(4 \times 13) \times 25 &= 4 \times (13 \times 25) && \text{associative property} \\
&= 4 \times (25 \times 13) && \text{commutative property} \\
&= (4 \times 25) \times 13 && \text{associative property} \\
&= 100 \times 13 = 1{,}300
\end{aligned}
$$

(b) The same three moves for addition bring 36 and 64 together: $(36 + 57) + 64 = 36 + (57 + 64) = 36 + (64 + 57) = (36 + 64) + 57 = 100 + 57 = 157$, using the associative, commutative and associative properties of addition.

(c) Write $102 = 100 + 2$ and distribute: $6 \times (100 + 2) = 6 \times 100 + 6 \times 2 = 600 + 12 = 612$.

Check by going left to right: $4 \times 13 = 52$ and $52 \times 25 = 1{,}300$, and $36 + 57 = 93$ and $93 + 64 = 157$. For (c), $5 \times 102 = 510$, and one more 102 makes 612.

</details>

### Standard

3\. Work out $9 \times 47 + 9 \times 53$ without working out $9 \times 47$ or $9 \times 53$. Name the property you use.

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Solution</summary>

Both products have the factor 9, so read the distributive property from right to left and pull the 9 out:

$$
9 \times 47 + 9 \times 53 = 9 \times (47 + 53) = 9 \times 100 = 900.
$$

Check the long way: $9 \times 47 = 423$ and $9 \times 53 = 477$, and $423 + 477 = 900$.

</details>

4\. A classmate says that division follows the same rules as multiplication, and writes (a) $(48 \div 4) \div 2 = 48 \div (4 \div 2)$ and (b) $60 \div (2 + 3) = 60 \div 2 + 60 \div 3$. Test each claim, and give the right value of each left side.

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Solution</summary>

(a) The left side is $12 \div 2 = 6$, and the right side is $48 \div 2 = 24$. They differ, so this is a counterexample to an associative property for division.

(b) The left side is $60 \div 5 = 12$, and the right side is $30 + 20 = 50$. They differ: dividing by a sum doesn't split.

The left sides are 6 and 12. Check by multiplying back: $6 \times 2 = 12$ and $12 \times 4 = 48$, and $12 \times 5 = 60$.

</details>

### Challenge

5\. Prove that $a \times 0 = 0$ for every number $a$. Use only the identity property of addition, the distributive property, and this fact: if a number $x$ satisfies $x + x = x$, then $x = 0$. (Hint: start from $0 + 0 = 0$.)

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Solution</summary>

We know the two properties and the fact about $x + x = x$. We want $a \times 0 = 0$ for every number $a$. The plan is to show that the number $a \times 0$ equals itself plus itself, so that the fact applies.

*Proof.* Let $a$ be a number, and call $x = a \times 0$. The identity property of addition, used with 0 as the number, says $0 + 0 = 0$. So

$$
\begin{aligned}
x = a \times 0 &= a \times (0 + 0) && \text{since } 0 + 0 = 0 \\
&= a \times 0 + a \times 0 && \text{distributive property} \\
&= x + x.
\end{aligned}
$$

So $x + x = x$, and the fact gives $x = 0$. That is, $a \times 0 = 0$.

Look back: the proof never picked a value for $a$, so it covers every number. The fact about $x + x = x$ is one subtraction away: take $x$ from both sides. Check one case: with $a = 7$, the middle line says $7 \times 0 = 7 \times 0 + 7 \times 0$, which is $0 = 0 + 0$, and that is true.

</details>

6\. This one reviews expanded form from [place value and rounding](01-place-value-and-rounding.md). Write 4,572 in expanded form (the value of each digit, added up). Then use the distributive property to work out $3 \times 4{,}572$ one place at a time.

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Solution</summary>

In expanded form, $4{,}572 = 4{,}000 + 500 + 70 + 2$. Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
3 \times 4{,}572 &= 3 \times (4{,}000 + 500 + 70 + 2) && \text{expanded form} \\
&= 3 \times 4{,}000 + 3 \times 500 + 3 \times 70 + 3 \times 2 && \text{distributive property} \\
&= 12{,}000 + 1{,}500 + 210 + 6 \\
&= 13{,}716.
\end{aligned}
$$

The distributive property is stated for a sum of two terms, but using it again and again covers four: in $3 \times (4{,}000 + (500 + (70 + 2)))$ it splits off one term at a time. The commutative and associative properties of addition then let you add the four pieces in whatever order is easiest, such as $12{,}000 + 1{,}500 = 13{,}500$ and $210 + 6 = 216$, then $13{,}500 + 216$.

Check: the estimate $3 \times 4{,}500 = 13{,}500$ is close to 13,716. This calculation is the column method of [multi-digit multiplication](05-multi-digit-multiplication.md), written out in full.

</details>

## Where this shows up

- Algebra runs on these properties. Expanding $2 \times (x + 3) = 2x + 6$ is the distributive property, and collecting like terms, $3x + 5x = (3 + 5)x = 8x$, is the same property read from right to left: [equivalent expressions](../02-middle-school/03-expressions-equations-inequalities/02-equivalent-expressions.md).
- The next lesson, [multi-digit multiplication](05-multi-digit-multiplication.md), uses the distributive property once for each place value.
- In code, check before you rearrange. Floating-point addition is not associative: in Python, `(0.1 + 0.2) + 0.3` gives `0.6000000000000001`, while `0.1 + (0.2 + 0.3)` gives `0.6` (floating point basics<!-- link: ../06-cs-math/01-number-representation/04-floating-point-basics.md -->). Joining strings is associative but not commutative, and so is matrix multiplication ([matrix algebra and inverses](../05-university-core/06-linear-algebra/03-matrix-algebra-and-inverses.md)).
